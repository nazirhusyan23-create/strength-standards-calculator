About Strength Standards

What this site does

Strength Standards is a small collection of 15 single-purpose calculators for barbell lifts and calisthenics movements. You enter a set you've actually performed — bodyweight, sex, and either a working set with reps or a hold time — and the calculator converts it into a bodyweight-adjusted strength ratio, then places that ratio into one of five tiers: Beginner, Novice, Intermediate, Advanced, or Elite.

The idea behind bodyweight ratios is simple: raw numbers on a barbell don't tell you much on their own. A 140 lb lifter deadlifting 245 lb and a 220 lb lifter deadlifting 385 lb are both pulling about 1.75× bodyweight — equally strong relative to their frame, even though the plates loaded are very different. Ranking by ratio instead of raw weight makes it possible to compare lifters of different sizes on a level footing.

How the numbers are calculated

For the six barbell-lift calculators (bench press, squat, deadlift, overhead press, weighted pull-up, weighted dip), a single working set of weight and reps is converted to an estimated one-rep max using the Epley formula (1RM = weight × (1 + reps ÷ 30), capped at 12 reps for accuracy), then divided by bodyweight to get the ratio used for tiering.

For the nine bodyweight and calisthenics calculators (pull-up, chin-up, dip, ring dip, push-up, muscle-up, pistol squat, handstand push-up, L-sit), reps or hold time are used directly, or converted to a bodyweight ratio the same way when added weight is involved — since a bodyweight movement doesn't always need external load to be a meaningful strength test.

All of the math — the 1RM estimate, the ratio, the tier lookup, and the progress toward the next tier — runs server-side in Python each time you submit a calculator. Nothing is pre-computed or approximated in the browser.

Where the tier thresholds come from

The Beginner-through-Elite breakpoints for each lift are assembled from commonly cited bodyweight-ratio strength standards and calisthenics progression benchmarks, cross-checked across a handful of published sources for consistency between lifts. They're a reasonable general reference point, not a scientific measurement of your individual capacity — genetics, training age, limb length, and technique all affect where an individual lifter's numbers actually land. See the disclaimer in the footer of every page, and treat your tier as a rough benchmark rather than a verdict.

Who's behind this

Strength Standards is an independently run reference site built and maintained by one person with an interest in strength training and calisthenics — not a gym, a supplement brand, or a certified coaching practice. It doesn't sell programs, coaching, or products; the calculators are free to use and the site earns a small amount from display advertising, which helps cover hosting.

Questions, corrections, or a threshold that looks off for a particular lift? Get in touch on the Contact page — corrections to the standards are always welcome.
